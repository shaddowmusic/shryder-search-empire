Four ways to search the city’s shelves

01

Tower Vinyl Shibuya: start broad

Tower Records identifies Tower Vinyl Shibuya as the sixth-floor analog specialist inside its Shibuya store. Current official material covers new and used vinyl, cassettes and record-related goods across multiple genres. Choose it when range and one-building simplicity matter.

  • Check the official Shibuya store page before travel.
  • Write a short genre and format list before entering a large floor.
  • Ask staff about listening and item-hold rules rather than assuming.

02

HMV record shop Shibuya: search the used bins

HMV’s official store page describes this two-floor Udagawacho shop as a used analog-record and CD specialist, with overseas buying and stock centered on releases from the 1960s through the 1990s alongside Japanese music shaped by those eras.

  • Use the dedicated record-shop page, not HMV&BOOKS Shibuya.
  • Treat rare-stock posts as snapshots; availability is never guaranteed.
  • Inspect the exact item, pressing and condition before purchase.

03

Disk Union Shinjuku: choose the genre first

Disk Union’s official Shinjuku directory separates stores and floors by intent, including jazz, soul and dance music, heavy metal, rock records, classical music, Japanese popular music, anime and game music, Latin and Brazilian music, punk, and independent or alternative rock.

  • Open the official area directory and pick the specialist first.
  • Do not assume every genre sits in one building or on one floor.
  • Recheck the specific shop page for current access and operating information.

04

Vinyl Delivery Service: make the records part of an art day

GO TOKYO’s current Kameari guide places Vinyl Delivery Service inside SKWAT Kameari Art Centre, alongside books, design and a café. The official guide reports about 8,000 in-store records, with genre-spanning buying connected to the owner’s London and Tokyo activity.

  • Choose this for the surrounding art-and-design context, not a rushed Shibuya add-on.
  • Some displayed records are described as online-only, so ask before assuming an item is available in store.
  • Check the official destination information for current access details.

Build the dig

Search with a system, not a sprint

  1. Choose the intent. Broad discovery, used records, a precise genre or an art-led visit create different routes.
  2. Write the want list. Artist, title, label, format and acceptable condition are more useful than “anything rare.”
  3. Verify the exact branch. HMV operates two differently named Shibuya stores, while Disk Union spreads specialist shops across Shinjuku.
  4. Check the physical item. Confirm pressing, media and sleeve condition, included inserts and the shop’s purchase terms before paying.
  5. Protect the journey home. Keep records upright, away from heat and pressure, and plan how the purchase will travel before adding more weight.

Useful restraint

One district can fill the day

Shibuya supports a close pairing of Tower Vinyl and HMV record shop. Shinjuku rewards genre specialists. Kameari is a separate east-Tokyo art route. Crossing all three weakens the search.

Common questions

District, format and condition

Which Tokyo record-shopping district should I choose?

Choose Shibuya for Tower Vinyl and HMV’s used-record shop, Shinjuku for multiple Disk Union genre specialists, or Kameari for Vinyl Delivery Service inside an art and design complex.

Where can I shop for used records in Tokyo?

HMV record shop Shibuya officially describes itself as a used analog-record and CD specialist. Disk Union also operates used and genre-specialist stores in Shinjuku. Inventory changes constantly, so check the current official information.

Should I visit several record districts in one day?

Usually one district is stronger. Serious browsing takes time, and Shibuya, Shinjuku and Kameari require different journeys. Choose one anchor and add only nearby stops.
